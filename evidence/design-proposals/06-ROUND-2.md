# 06 — The second round, read for topology

The first round answered the *navigation* question and missed the *spatial*
one. Four authors, four metaphors, one application — and one skeleton: a column
at the left, a list in the middle, a card at the right, a bar at the bottom.
Every image was maximalised. Not one region was allowed to be empty. Four
metaphors, four coats of paint.

So the second round changed the terms. The metaphors were **crossed** — each
author built the idea of another, with their own tools and their own taste —
and the brief forbade the inherited shape, capped the information on the
surface at eight kinds, and asked for **two screens per author**: the loaded
one and the quiet one, the state right after opening, before any search.

Eight images. This is what they say, and what they cost.

## The four, in one line each

| | The metaphor | The arrangement it produced |
|---|---|---|
| **01 L'Instrument** | a package manager as a precision instrument | the *faceplate*: a single milled plate read from the top down in full-width bands — header, source bank, control strip, readout window, specification nameplate, record tape. The sources are a horizontal **bank** of seven apertures (a range selector); the operation is one machined **rotary** in a bay on the right. The detail is the readout's caption, a full-width nameplate. The log is a paper tape emerging from a slot. |
| **02 La Carte** | a package manager as a chart | the *sheet*: one continuous chart. A title margin (mark, name, bearing rose, query, freshness), the chart itself (land, coast, points, soundings, scales, an inset), a key band naming the seven territories, and a full-width table of points. The detail is an **inset drawn on the chart** with a leader line, not a card at the right. The log is the **ship's track** on the water. |
| **03 L'Atelier** | a package manager as a workbench | the *bench seen from above*: a **rack of seven trays above** the bench is the source axis (you pull one down; the other six are shut, and their stencilled lips state the non-interchangeability before you ask), a ruled mat in the middle is the work, and a cork rail at the near edge is the log. The operation is **chalked on the mat** — the job — not a control among controls. |
| **04 Le Comptoir** | a package manager as a shop counter at night | the *counter*: a hanging sign with the mark, the seven sources as **counters on a board across the top wall** (a lamp, a name, a word for what it holds, a count), the packages as **objects on a shelf** below, and the operation as a **till** — one physical switch standing in the foreground. The selected package's ticket is propped beside it, the request is chalked on a slate, the backend's output prints on a receipt. |

## The topologies, side by side

This is the comparison that matters. Not the paint — the arrangement.

| | **L'Instrument** | **La Carte** | **L'Atelier** | **Le Comptoir** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The seven sources** | a horizontal bank of seven apertures, each stating its kind (`BIN`, `SRC`, `SBX`, `ONE`, `CRATE`, `NODE`, `GIT`) and its count | a key band at the bottom, naming what each territory holds, how much is charted, and which two hold no index | a rack of seven trays across the top; the lit one is open, the others shut | a board of seven counters across the top wall, each with a lamp and a count |
| **The three operations** | one rotary knob in a bay on the right, three engraved detents | a compass rose in the title margin, the bearing written on it | chalked on the mat — the job, in handwriting | a till — a physical switch that moves between its positions |
| **The list** | the readout window, cut into the plate, ruled, with the delta measured as bars on a calibrated scale | the chart — the packages are points, the size is the depth, on a log scale | the ruled mat: a table of tools, with provenance, size, state, and when it was placed | a shelf of **objects** — five, lit by a lamp, each with a price tag |
| **The detail** | a full-width **nameplate** of engraved cells under the readout | an **inset drawn on the chart**, tied to its point by a leader line | a card **laid loose on the mat** beside the tool you pick up | a **ticket** propped on the counter, in the foreground plane |
| **The log** | a perforated **paper tape** at the bottom | the **ship's track**, a timeline on the water | a cork **rail** at the near edge | a **receipt** running out of the frame, bottom left |
| **The empty region** | the readout window, uncalibrated glass — *the author's own doubt: it reads as an error, not as a dial* | the water below the coast, charted but not yet plotted | the lower mat: engraved grid, one dashed slot saying which package would fit, the outlined place of the card | the counter's middle, the space the ticket will occupy — *and it moves in the loaded state* |
| **The keyboard** | behind one engraved `?` | absent from the sheet | absent | absent |
| **The mark** | top left, large | top left, in the margin | top left | on the hanging sign, top left, in its own slab |

Three of the four moved the sources **out of the left column** — into a band,
a rack, a board. That is the single clearest result of the round: the first
round's most inherited gesture was the vertical source rail, and it did not
survive being argued about.

## What the round settles

1. **The sources want to be horizontal, and they want to state their kind.**
   Three of the four put the seven sources in a band across the top, and every
   one of them prints what the source *is* next to its count — `BIN`, `SRC`,
   `SBX`, `ONE`, `CRATE`, `NODE`, `GIT`. The non-interchangeability is no
   longer a note in a legend; it is the first thing the eye reads.
2. **The two axes must not look alike, not merely not share a control.** A bank
   of seven equal positions and a single three-position rotary are different
   *kinds* of thing. A chalked job and a till switch are different kinds of
   thing. The rule is now visual, not just structural.
3. **The detail does not have to be a card in a column.** It can be a caption
   under the readout, an inset drawn on the sheet, a card laid on the mat, a
   ticket on the counter. The first round's "a card at the right" was an
   accident of the layout, not a requirement of the product.
4. **The log does not have to be a bar.** It can be a tape, a track, a rail, a
   receipt. It is the least structural of the four elements and the one the
   four authors placed most differently — which suggests it should be the
   freest.
5. **A quiet state is a real test, and two of the four passed it.** The Atelier
   and the Carte compose their emptiness (an engraved grid with a slot that
   says what would fit; a coast that is charted before it is plotted). The
   Instrument's empty window reads as a missing panel — its author says so
   himself. The Comptoir's emptiness moves between states, which is the failure
   its own question warns about.

## What it does not settle

1. **Is the list a list?** The Comptoir draws the packages as five *objects on
   a shelf* — which is cards, the thing the product explicitly refused. The
   others keep a table, and the Carte's own note is the sharpest question of
   the round: *have I found a way to show the state of a package manager that
   is not a table? I changed the furniture, not the fact of it.* A coastline of
   points is still a table of points.
2. **Does the user ever choose a source on purpose?** The Atelier's question,
   and it is the deepest one: if a person thinks "I want a PDF tool" and the
   source is a detail the product resolves, then a third of every surface in
   this round is a category the user does not have. Nothing in the round
   answers it. It is a product question, not a design question.
3. **How much of the instrument survives a real session?** The Instrument is the
   most beautiful and the densest: the scope strip, the permits, the forbids,
   the actions, the count, the filter and the two legends all sit on the plate
   at once. It claims eight kinds of information; the plate shows closer to
   eleven. The claim is the interesting part, not the compliance.

## The four questions, and the answers the product already has

The authors left one question each. Three of the four are already answered by
decisions the product has made; one is not.

- *When an operation does not exist for the source the user is on, is the
  answer to remove it, to disable it, or to say "there is nothing here"?*
  **Answered: the absence of an action expresses the limit, and the reason
  lives in the source's own entry.** The Instrument's own plate says it best —
  `BUILD — NOTHING TO BUILD` and `NO RECIPE HERE` — which is the third
  answer, and it is the right one.
- *Is the state of a package manager showable without a table?* **Unanswered,
  and it is the right question.** The answer decides whether the next slice is
  a layout or a vocabulary.
- *Does a person choosing software ever consciously choose a source?* **The
  honest answer is "rarely, and the product should not pretend otherwise"** —
  which is why the sources are one band, always visible, never a filter the
  user has to think about.
- *When your surface is empty, is the space you leave bare the same space your
  content will later fill?* **Yes, and the quiet screens are how you find
  out.** Two of the four failed it.

## Two fidelity gaps, in the images themselves

- **The log prints `sudo pacman`.** The product never does that. It shows
  `calix install standard <name>` as its own command, and the log underneath
  shows what the backend actually ran — through polkit, without `sudo`. Three
  of the four proposals print `sudo`; it is a small thing, and it is wrong.
- **The Comptoir's shelf is cards.** The product asked for a detailed list and
  a compact list, and the compact one is explicitly *not* a grid of cards. The
  objects on the shelf are cards with a metaphor on top.

## What I would take, and what I would not

I am not going to pick a winner from four images, and I would be dishonest if
I pretended the round produced one. What it produced is a set of decisions
that survive independently of the metaphor that carried them:

1. **The sources leave the left column** and become a horizontal band across
   the top, each source stating its kind and its count. (Instrument, Atelier,
   Comptoir; the Carte's key band is the same idea at the other end of the
   sheet.)
2. **The operation leaves the surface's control row** and becomes one object
   of a different kind — a rotary, a rose, a chalked job, a till.
3. **The detail stops being a right-hand card** and becomes a caption, an
   inset, a card on the mat, or a ticket — chosen per arrangement, with the
   arrangement in mind.
4. **The log stops being a bar** and becomes the freest of the four elements.
5. **One region is left empty on purpose, and it is the *same* region in both
   states.** The quiet screen is the acceptance test, not an afterthought.
6. **The keyboard leaves the surface** — every one of the four put it behind a
   help overlay, none printed a key map.

And what I would not take: the card-shaped list (the Comptoir), the empty
window that reads as an error (the Instrument), and the horizon that cannot
be reached (the Carte's) — the last one is a beautiful idea that says
"unreachable" about sources the user *can* reach with a different bearing.

## The gaps in this reading

- The four images are renders, not the application. Nothing here is a
  screenshot of Calix; the authors were forbidden from looking at it.
- The round produced no proof that any of these arrangements *works* — only
  that they are thinkable. The next step is not another image.
- The question of the table is still open, and it is the one that decides
  whether the requalification is a layout or a vocabulary.
